# Initial Landscape and Failure-Mode Report

**Evidence snapshot: 22 October 2026.** This is a focused initial landscape, not a systematic review. Search scope and limitations are recorded in the [literature-search protocol](LITERATURE-SEARCH-PROTOCOL.md). Clinical-trial status can change; verify the linked official record before acting on it.

## Executive summary

No established cure for T1D is identified. The strongest near-term clinical signal in this snapshot is beta-cell replacement: donor-islet therapy has a narrow FDA-approved indication for selected adults with recurrent severe hypoglycemia, and a small phase 1–2 stem-cell-derived islet study reported insulin independence in 10 of 12 full-dose recipients at day 365. The paper reports 14 participants with at least 12 months of follow-up (2 in part A and 12 full-dose participants in parts B/C); all 12 full-dose participants were free of severe hypoglycemic events during days 90–365 and had HbA1c below 7%, and 10/12 were insulin-independent at day 365. These analyses were interim and not prespecified. Safety must be considered alongside efficacy: serious neutropenia occurred in 3/14 participants, and two deaths were reported during follow-up (2/14): one caused by cryptococcal meningitis and one due to progression of preexisting neurocognitive impairment. Reporting an event and its cause does not establish treatment causality. [S3]

A 2026 follow-up reports continued graft function and no detected immune response through 14 months in the same single recipient of gene-edited donor islets without immunosuppression. This strengthens the duration of the observed graft-function signal, but it does not demonstrate insulin independence and cannot establish efficacy, long-term safety, or generalizability. [S5, S8]

An important negative result is the discontinued VX-264 encapsulation program. Vertex reported that the phase 1/2 study did not meet its efficacy endpoint and the program would not advance. The cited update does not establish the precise biological or device-related explanation; it said further analysis was planned. This is an informative program-level result, not proof that all encapsulation strategies cannot work. [S9, S10]

Immune therapies such as teplizumab delay disease onset or decline in endogenous insulin production for defined groups; they do not restore normal beta-cell mass or cure established T1D. [S6]

## Evidence map

### 1. Islet replacement from deceased donors

FDA approved donislecel-jujn (Lantidra) in 2023 for adults with T1D who cannot approach target glucose control because of current repeated severe hypoglycemia despite intensive management and education. The FDA describes this as a treatment option for a narrow population. This is not a general cure: supply is limited, transplantation is invasive, and immunosuppression creates significant risks. [S1, S2]

### 2. Stem-cell-derived islets with immunosuppression

The 2025 phase 1–2 zimislecel study reported 14 participants with at least 12 months of follow-up: 2 received a half dose in part A, and 12 received a full dose in parts B/C. The published part C primary endpoint combined freedom from severe hypoglycemic events during days 90–365 with an HbA1c threshold (below 7% or at least a 1-percentage-point decrease from baseline at one or more assessments between days 180–365). All 12 full-dose participants were free of severe hypoglycemic events and had HbA1c below 7%; the abstract also reports that all 12 spent more than 70% of time in the target glucose range (70–180 mg/dL), without separately specifying the aggregation interval for time in range. Insulin independence was a secondary outcome: 10/12 full-dose participants were not using exogenous insulin at day 365. All 14 had undetectable C-peptide at baseline and detectable C-peptide after infusion; the abstract does not specify a single post-infusion assessment time. All analyses were interim and not prespecified. These results are encouraging but remain small, short-term, and uncontrolled; insulin independence under immunosuppression is not a cure. [S3]

Neutropenia was the most common serious adverse event and occurred in 3/14 participants in the 14-person follow-up population. Two deaths were reported during follow-up (2/14): one caused by cryptococcal meningitis and one due to progression of preexisting neurocognitive impairment. These events and their reported causes should be distinguished from conclusions about treatment causality. Participants received glucocorticoid-free systemic immunosuppressive therapy; the approach therefore retains immunosuppression-related risks. [S3]

ClinicalTrials.gov record NCT04786262 was queried through the official API on 22 October 2026 and showed RECRUITING. The overall record field lists Phase 3 and includes VX-880 and VX-017 arms; its latest posted version was 1 September 2026 and the status was verified in August 2026. Current registry outcomes list safety follow-up for parts A and D through at least 5 years and an insulin-independence/severe-hypoglycemia outcome for parts B/C one year after achieving insulin independence. The published 2025 paper reports the earlier VX-880 phase 1–2 study, not VX-017 outcomes; the current registry record has no posted results section. Do not conflate the published study endpoints with the current master-protocol registry fields. Registry status describes the record, not whether any particular location is open or a person is eligible; confirm both with study contacts. [S4]

### 3. Immune-evasive/hypoimmune cells

A 2025 report describes genetically modified donor islets (not stem-cell-derived islets) transplanted into one person with longstanding T1D without immunosuppressive drugs; no immune response against the edited cells was detected at 12 weeks. A July 2026 NEJM follow-up in the same person reports evidence of beta-cell function and no detected immune response through 14 months. This remains a single-participant proof of concept and does not demonstrate insulin independence. Long-term safety, residual immune biology, manufacturing, and generalizability remain open questions. The associated official registry record NCT06239636 was queried on 22 October 2026 and showed RECRUITING, but its latest posted version was 11 December 2024 and its status was verified in December 2024. The record lists an estimated enrollment of 2 and a 12-month safety endpoint; its status may not reflect current site availability. Confirm directly with study contacts. [S5, S7, S8]

### 4. Encapsulation: VX-264 negative result

VX-264 combined stem-cell-derived islets with an implantable device designed to avoid immunosuppressive medication. In a March 2025 update, Vertex said the Phase 1/2 study did not meet its efficacy endpoint and the program would not advance. The cited update did not disclose the analyzed denominator or endpoint-level outcome counts, and it does not identify a definitive mechanism of failure; further analyses were planned. On 22 October 2026, the official ClinicalTrials.gov record listed NCT05791201 as ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING; its latest posted version was 9 October 2026 and its status was verified in October 2026. The record lists actual enrollment of 7 and outcomes for safety through 24 months and change in peak C-peptide from baseline to day 90; no results section was posted. The registry label does not reverse the sponsor’s decision to stop advancing the program. Track the negative result and its uncertainty; do not generalize this program outcome to every encapsulation design. Site availability and eligibility must be confirmed with study contacts. [S9, S10]

### 5. Disease-modifying immune therapy

In June 2026, FDA granted accelerated approval for teplizumab to delay the decline in endogenous insulin production in certain children ages 8–17 recently diagnosed with stage 3 T1D. FDA states approval relied on C-peptide, a surrogate endpoint reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit, and a postapproval study is required. The labeling includes a boxed warning for serious/life-threatening viral reactivation. This is preservation/delay, not a cure. Teplizumab also has a separate stage 2 indication to delay onset; keep populations and endpoints distinct. [S6]

### 6. Regeneration and immune tolerance

These remain active research directions, but this first sprint has not completed a comprehensive primary-source review of individual programs. Evaluate whether each approach addresses beta-cell supply, immune recurrence, function, durability, safety, and scalability together. No broad efficacy claim is made here.

## Recurring failure modes and bottlenecks

| Failure mode | Why it matters | Evidence to track |
|---|---|---|
| Autoimmune recurrence | Replaced cells may be attacked by the same autoreactive immune process. | Autoantibodies, cellular immune response, graft function, disease-stage stratification. |
| Alloimmune rejection | Donor-derived cells can trigger host immune rejection. | HLA matching/editing, graft survival, immunosuppression, immune monitoring. |
| Immunosuppression burden | Infection, malignancy, organ toxicity, and monitoring can offset benefit. | Specific regimen, adverse events, cumulative exposure, discontinuation. |
| Engraftment and cell function | Cells must survive, vascularize/oxygenate, sense glucose, and secrete appropriately. | C-peptide, glucose responsiveness, time-in-range, graft survival and location. |
| Durability and overclaiming | One-year insulin independence does not establish multi-year stable function. | Denominators, follow-up, insulin use, HbA1c/time-in-range, severe hypoglycemia. |
| Encapsulation/device performance | A device may not sustain sufficient cell engraftment or function, as the VX-264 program-level endpoint failure illustrates; the mechanism remains unresolved. | C-peptide, oxygenation, fibrosis, cell survival, explant/device analysis, device-specific adverse events. |
| Manufacturing and consistency | Scale-up, purity, potency, batch consistency, and cost constrain access. | Product characterization, release criteria, manufacturing deviations, delivery capacity. |
| Tumor/off-target risk | Pluripotent-cell products require careful assessment of residual/undesired cells. | Long-term surveillance, product purity, genomic stability, tumorigenicity. |
| Trial selection/generalizability | Early studies often enroll highly selected participants with severe disease. | Inclusion criteria, demographics, comparator, site availability, external validity. |
| Endpoint mismatch | Surrogate or short-term endpoints may not equal durable patient benefit. | Pre-specified endpoint, clinical importance, independent replication, postapproval evidence. |
| Access and treatment burden | Complex procedures may be unavailable or unaffordable. | Eligibility, geography, cost, donor/cell supply, patient-reported burden. |

## Research priorities for Sprint 01

1. Reconcile each active registry with its protocol and linked results/publications.
2. Compare replacement strategies by insulin independence, C-peptide, severe hypoglycemia, glycemic outcomes, immunosuppression, adverse events, and follow-up.
3. Separate immune-evasive allogeneic donor cells from stem-cell-derived products; do not infer between them.
4. Build an explicit failure/negative-results ledger for encapsulation and immune modulation, including the VX-264 endpoint failure and what remains unknown.
5. Define a minimum “functional cure” outcome set and avoid using the term for studies that do not meet it.
