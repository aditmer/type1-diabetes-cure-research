# Initial Landscape and Failure-Mode Report

**Evidence snapshot: 22 October 2026.** This is a focused initial landscape, not a systematic review. Search scope and limitations are recorded in the [literature-search protocol](LITERATURE-SEARCH-PROTOCOL.md). Clinical-trial status can change; verify the linked official record before acting on it.

For a focused comparison of deceased-donor, allogeneic stem-cell-derived, autologous CiPSC-derived, gene-edited immune-evasive donor, and encapsulated stem-cell-derived islets, see the [five-strategy comparison](CELL-REPLACEMENT-COMPARISON.md).

## Executive summary

No established cure for T1D is identified. The strongest near-term clinical signal in this snapshot is beta-cell replacement: donor-islet therapy has a narrow FDA-approved indication for selected adults with recurrent severe hypoglycemia, and a small phase 1–2 stem-cell-derived islet study reported insulin independence in 10 of 12 full-dose recipients at day 365. The paper reports 14 participants with at least 12 months of follow-up (2 in part A and 12 full-dose participants in parts B/C); all 12 full-dose participants were free of severe hypoglycemic events during days 90–365 and had HbA1c below 7%, and 10/12 were insulin-independent at day 365. These analyses were interim and not prespecified. Safety must be considered alongside efficacy: serious neutropenia occurred in 3/14 participants, and two deaths were reported during follow-up (2/14): one caused by cryptococcal meningitis and one due to progression of preexisting neurocognitive impairment. Reporting an event and its cause does not establish treatment causality. [S3]

A 2026 follow-up reports continued graft function and no detected immune response through 14 months in the same single recipient of gene-edited donor islets without immunosuppression. This strengthens the duration of the observed graft-function signal, but it does not demonstrate insulin independence and cannot establish efficacy, long-term safety, or generalizability. [S5, S8]

Preclinical hypoimmune-engineering studies report immune evasion and graft survival in humanized mice and non-human primates. In one study, human iPSC-derived hypoimmune islet cells improved glycemia through 28 days in diabetic humanized mice, and edited primary rhesus islets survived for 40 weeks in one allogeneic macaque without immunosuppression. A separate study reported insulin independence in one diabetic non-human primate after transplantation of hypoimmune pseudo-islets; its accessible abstract does not specify follow-up duration. These are animal-model findings, not human efficacy evidence. The primate iPSC experiments also produced teratomatous growths from undifferentiated cells, underscoring that survival/immune evasion does not resolve cell-product safety. [S14, S15]

Encapsulation/device evidence is mixed and design-specific. The official VC-01/Encaptra record reports termination for insufficient functional product engraftment; the planned efficacy cohort was never enrolled, so the registry does not identify the mechanism. Open VC-02/PEC-Direct devices allowed vascular ingrowth and showed C-peptide production in small human study groups, but required systemic immunosuppression and have not demonstrated insulin independence. Vertex reported that VX-264 did not meet its efficacy endpoint and would not advance; the sponsor update did not establish a definitive failure mechanism. These program outcomes do not prove that all encapsulation or immune-evasion designs cannot work. [S17, S18, S19, S20, S9, S10]

Immune therapies such as teplizumab delay disease onset or decline in endogenous insulin production for defined groups; they do not restore normal beta-cell mass or cure established T1D. [S6]

## Evidence map

### 1. Islet replacement from deceased donors

FDA approved donislecel-jujn (Lantidra) in 2023 for adults with T1D who cannot approach target glucose control because of current repeated severe hypoglycemia despite intensive management and education. The FDA describes this as a treatment option for a narrow population. This is not a general cure: supply is limited, transplantation is invasive, and immunosuppression creates significant risks. [S1, S2]

### 2. Stem-cell-derived islets with immunosuppression

The 2025 phase 1–2 zimislecel study reported 14 participants with at least 12 months of follow-up: 2 received a half dose in part A, and 12 received a full dose in parts B/C. The published part C primary endpoint combined freedom from severe hypoglycemic events during days 90–365 with an HbA1c threshold (below 7% or at least a 1-percentage-point decrease from baseline at one or more assessments between days 180–365). The abstract reports all 12 full-dose participants were free of severe hypoglycemic events and had HbA1c below 7%, but does not explicitly establish the assessment time for each participant's reported HbA1c result; the endpoint's defined window should not be assumed to be the observed result's timing without checking the full report or supporting tables. The abstract also reports that all 12 spent more than 70% of time in the target glucose range (70–180 mg/dL), without separately specifying the aggregation interval for time in range. Insulin independence was a secondary outcome: 10/12 full-dose participants were not using exogenous insulin at day 365. All 14 had undetectable C-peptide at baseline and detectable C-peptide after infusion; the abstract does not specify a single post-infusion assessment time. All analyses were interim and not prespecified. These results are encouraging but remain small, short-term, and uncontrolled; insulin independence under immunosuppression is not a cure. [S3]

Neutropenia was the most common serious adverse event and occurred in 3/14 participants in the 14-person follow-up population. Two deaths were reported during follow-up (2/14): one caused by cryptococcal meningitis and one due to progression of preexisting neurocognitive impairment. These events and their reported causes should be distinguished from conclusions about treatment causality. Participants received glucocorticoid-free systemic immunosuppressive therapy; the approach therefore retains immunosuppression-related risks. [S3]

ClinicalTrials.gov record NCT04786262 was queried through the official API on 22 October 2026 and showed RECRUITING. The overall record field lists Phase 3 and includes VX-880 and VX-017 arms; its latest posted version was 1 September 2026 and the status was verified in August 2026. Current registry outcomes list safety follow-up for parts A and D through at least 5 years and an insulin-independence/severe-hypoglycemia outcome for parts B/C one year after achieving insulin independence. The published 2025 paper reports the earlier VX-880 phase 1–2 study, not VX-017 outcomes; the current registry record has no posted results section. Do not conflate the published study endpoints with the current master-protocol registry fields. Registry status describes the record, not whether any particular location is open or a person is eligible; confirm both with study contacts. [S4]

### 3. Immune-evasive/hypoimmune cells

A 2025 report describes genetically modified donor islets (not stem-cell-derived islets) transplanted into one person with longstanding T1D without immunosuppressive drugs; no immune response against the edited cells was detected at 12 weeks. A July 2026 NEJM follow-up in the same person reports evidence of beta-cell function and no detected immune response through 14 months. This remains a single-participant proof of concept and does not demonstrate insulin independence. Long-term safety, residual immune biology, manufacturing, and generalizability remain open questions. The associated official registry record NCT06239636 was queried on 22 October 2026 and showed RECRUITING, but its latest posted version was 11 December 2024 and its status was verified in December 2024. The record lists an estimated enrollment of 2 and a 12-month safety endpoint; its status may not reflect current site availability. Confirm directly with study contacts. [S5, S7, S8]

Separate primary preclinical programs use hypoimmune engineering of primary donor islets or stem-cell-derived cells. In humanized diabetic mice, HIP islets survived through day 28 and reduced fasting glucose toward 200 mg/dL; in a separate allogeneic rhesus macaque, edited primary islets survived for 40 weeks without immunosuppression. Another single-animal primate report describes insulin independence after transplantation of hypoimmune pseudo-islets. These results support mechanism-level feasibility only. Species/model differences, small animal numbers, limited follow-up, and the absence of human efficacy data prevent extrapolation to people with T1D. [S14, S15, S16]

The primary human-islet study reports that edited primary islet pseudoislets survived and improved diabetes in allogeneic humanized mice, avoided autoimmune killing in autologous autoimmune humanized mice, and could be eliminated with a CD47-targeting antibody, which the authors propose as a safety strategy. Only the abstract was accessible, so sample sizes and durations are not extracted. This is a mouse-model observation; it does not show that an anti-CD47 removal approach would be effective, complete, selective, or safe in humans. [S16]

The same primate study found that undifferentiated hypoimmune iPSCs could form teratomatous, multi-lineage growths after implantation. This finding concerns undifferentiated iPSCs, not a clinical trial of a purified, differentiated islet product, but it illustrates why long-term tumorigenicity, off-target differentiation, genomic stability, and product-release controls remain essential. [S14]

### 4. Encapsulation and device strategies

**Closed immune-protective macroencapsulation (VC-01/Encaptra).** The official ClinicalTrials.gov record for NCT02239354 lists the phase 1/2 study as TERMINATED for “insufficient functional product engraftment.” It reports 19 actual participants in Cohort 1; 9 completed and 10 did not. Cohort 2 was never enrolled, so the planned C-peptide efficacy endpoint has no data. The registry does not establish that fibrosis or oxygen limitation was the specific cause. This is a negative device/program result, not proof that all closed encapsulation designs fail. [S17]

**Open, vascularized macroencapsulation (VC-02/PEC-Direct).** Unlike a closed immunoprotective device, the perforated device permits host vessel ingrowth to support cell survival but also permits immune-cell entry; participants therefore received systemic immunosuppression. In a first-in-human report, 17 participants received PEC-01 cells in VC-02; 6/17 had positive C-peptide, detected as early as six months, and engraftment/insulin expression was observed in 63% of explanted device units at 3–12 months. This is evidence of graft function, not insulin independence. [S18]

An optimized NCT03163511 study group received higher cell doses and a changed membrane-perforation pattern. In its interim one-year report, the primary endpoint was meal-stimulated C-peptide above the assay detection limit (0.03 nmol/L) at month 6, met by 4/10 participants with undetectable baseline C-peptide. This detectable-C-peptide outcome is distinct from, and nests the higher threshold below: 3 of those 4 (3/10) achieved meal-stimulated levels of at least 0.1 nmol/L from month 6 onwards, with associated CGM improvements and insulin-dose reductions. Beta-cell mass in a sentinel device from the participant with the strongest response was 4% of initial cell mass at month 6. Two of 10 participants had treatment-emergent serious adverse events: one attributed to surgery and one to protocol immunosuppression. These publications are related reports from the same registry program, not independent trials; neither establishes insulin independence or a functional cure. The registry lists 49 total actual participants and posted results, including a Week 26 C-peptide analysis of 42 participants. [S18, S19, S20]

**Prevascularized donor-islet devices.** The Sernova Cell Pouch record describes scaffold prevascularization followed by allogeneic donor-islet transplantation under systemic immunosuppression. As of the 22 October 2026 query, NCT03513939 was ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING, with an estimated enrollment of 17 and no results posted. This is a registered protocol, not evidence of benefit. Encellin's NCT06408311 is a phase 1 device-feasibility study of macroencapsulated primary donor islets; its primary outcome is device-related adverse events and secondary outcomes include explant containment and fibrosis. The record was ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING, estimated enrollment 10, with no results posted. Protocols and status snapshots do not establish observed engraftment or efficacy. [S21, S22]

**VX-264.** This program combined stem-cell-derived islets with a device intended to avoid immunosuppressive medication. Vertex reported in March 2025 that the phase 1/2 study did not meet its efficacy endpoint and would not advance. The cited sponsor update does not disclose the analyzed denominator or endpoint-level counts, nor establish a definitive mechanism. On 22 October 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov listed NCT05791201 as ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING; the record was last posted 9 October 2026, status verified in October, with 7 actual participants and no results posted. Registry state does not reverse the sponsor's development decision. [S9, S10]

### 5. Disease-modifying immune therapy

In June 2026, FDA granted accelerated approval for teplizumab to delay the decline in endogenous insulin production in certain children ages 8–17 recently diagnosed with stage 3 T1D. FDA states approval relied on C-peptide, a surrogate endpoint reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit, and a postapproval study is required. The labeling includes a boxed warning for serious/life-threatening viral reactivation. This is preservation/delay, not a cure. Teplizumab also has a separate stage 2 indication to delay onset; keep populations and endpoints distinct. [S6]

### 6. Regeneration and immune tolerance

These remain active research directions, but this first sprint has not completed a comprehensive primary-source review of individual programs. Evaluate whether each approach addresses beta-cell supply, immune recurrence, function, durability, safety, and scalability together. No broad efficacy claim is made here.

The ranked, source-linked matrix is maintained in [`data/failure-modes.csv`](../data/failure-modes.csv); it distinguishes product-specific observations, hypothesized mechanisms, and explicit evidence gaps. The [Sprint 02 research-priority memo](SPRINT-02-RESEARCH-PRIORITIES.md) explains the scoring and identifies candidate falsifiable questions. This report does not maintain a second copy of the matrix.

## Research priorities for Sprint 01

1. Reconcile registry records against linked publications/results and clearly distinguish current status from stale verification dates and sponsor program decisions.
2. Compare immune-evasive donor cells, hypoimmune stem-cell-derived products, closed devices, open vascularized devices, and prevascularized scaffolds without pooling models or cohorts.
3. Track graft function, immune response, oxygenation/vascularization, fibrosis, cell mass, immunosuppression, adverse events, and follow-up; preserve unknowns where sources do not report measurements.
4. Maintain an explicit failure/negative-results ledger for VC-01 and VX-264 while avoiding modality-wide conclusions from individual programs.
5. Define a minimum “functional cure” outcome set and avoid using the term for studies that do not meet it.
