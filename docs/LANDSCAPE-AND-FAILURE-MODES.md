# Initial Landscape and Failure-Mode Report

**Evidence snapshot: 22 October 2026.** This is a first-pass map, not a systematic review. The focus is what evidence currently supports and what it does not. Clinical-trial status can change; verify the linked official record before acting on it.

## Executive summary

No established cure for T1D is identified. The strongest near-term clinical signal in this snapshot is beta-cell replacement: donor-islet therapy has a narrow FDA-approved indication for selected adults with recurrent severe hypoglycemia, and an early phase 1–2 stem-cell-derived islet study reported insulin independence in 10 of 12 full-dose recipients at day 365. These outcomes are meaningful but do not establish a broadly applicable or durable cure: the studies are small/selected, follow-up is limited, and cell replacement generally carries immunosuppression or other major constraints.

Immune-evasive transplantation is an important but very early line: one genetically modified donor-islet recipient was reported without immunosuppression and with no detected immune response at 12 weeks. A single participant and short follow-up cannot establish safety, durability, efficacy, or scalability. Immune therapies such as teplizumab delay disease onset or decline in endogenous insulin production for defined groups; they do not restore normal beta-cell mass or cure established T1D.

## Evidence map

### 1. Islet replacement from deceased donors

FDA approved donislecel-jujn (Lantidra) in 2023 for adults with T1D who cannot approach target glucose control because of current repeated severe hypoglycemia despite intensive management and education. The FDA describes this as a treatment option for a narrow population. The label and FDA review should be checked for specific efficacy and adverse-event details. This is not a general cure: supply is limited, transplantation is invasive, and immunosuppression creates significant risks. [S1, S2]

### 2. Stem-cell-derived islets with immunosuppression

The 2025 phase 1–2 zimislecel study reported 14 participants with at least 12 months of follow-up across study parts; 10 of 12 full-dose recipients were insulin-independent at day 365. The authors characterize the results as small and short-term and call for further investigation. The study addresses replacement of beta-cell function; systemic immunosuppression remained part of the approach, so it does not establish immune tolerance or a medication-free cure. [S3]

Official registry record NCT04786262 was queried on 22 October 2026 and showed RECRUITING. The study includes zimislecel/VX-880 and VX-017 arms. A registry status describes the record, not whether any particular location is open or whether a person is eligible. [S4]

### 3. Immune-evasive/hypoimmune cells

A 2025 report describes genetically modified donor islets transplanted into one person with longstanding T1D without immunosuppressive drugs; no immune response against the edited cells was detected at 12 weeks. This is proof-of-concept human evidence, not efficacy or cure evidence. Follow-up, participant count, residual immune biology, cell source, manufacturing, and long-term safety remain major constraints. The associated official registry record NCT06239636 was queried on 22 October 2026 and showed RECRUITING; its estimated enrollment is very small and a registry label does not ensure a site is open. [S5, S7]

### 4. Disease-modifying immune therapy

In June 2026, FDA granted accelerated approval for teplizumab to delay the decline in endogenous insulin production in certain children ages 8–17 recently diagnosed with stage 3 T1D. FDA states approval relied on C-peptide, a surrogate endpoint reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit, and a postapproval study is required. The labeling includes a boxed warning for serious/life-threatening viral reactivation. This is preservation/delay, not a cure. Teplizumab also has a separate stage 2 indication to delay onset; keep the populations and endpoints distinct. [S6]

### 5. Encapsulation, regeneration, and immune tolerance

These remain active research directions, but the first sprint has not completed a comprehensive primary-source review of individual programs. The key question is whether they can solve beta-cell supply, oxygenation/vascularization, immune escape or tolerance, function, durability, and safety together. No broad efficacy claim is made here; Sprint 01 issues assign structured review before inclusion in the evidence tracker.

## Recurring failure modes and bottlenecks

| Failure mode | Why it matters | Evidence to track |
|---|---|---|
| Autoimmune recurrence | Replaced cells may be attacked by the same autoreactive immune process. | Autoantibodies, cellular immune response, graft function, disease-stage stratification. |
| Alloimmune rejection | Donor-derived cells can trigger host immune rejection. | HLA matching/editing, graft survival, immunosuppression, immune monitoring. |
| Immunosuppression burden | Infection, malignancy, organ toxicity, and monitoring can offset benefit. | Specific regimen, adverse events, cumulative exposure, discontinuation. |
| Engraftment and cell function | Cells must survive, vascularize/oxygenate, sense glucose, and secrete appropriately. | C-peptide, glucose responsiveness, time-in-range, graft survival and location. |
| Durability and overclaiming | One-year insulin independence does not establish multi-year stable function. | Denominators, follow-up, insulin use, HbA1c/time-in-range, severe hypoglycemia. |
| Manufacturing and consistency | Scale-up, purity, potency, batch consistency, and cost constrain access. | Product characterization, release criteria, manufacturing deviations, delivery capacity. |
| Tumor/off-target risk | Pluripotent-cell products require careful assessment of residual/undesired cells. | Long-term surveillance, product purity, genomic stability, tumorigenicity. |
| Trial selection/generalizability | Early studies often enroll highly selected participants with severe disease. | Inclusion criteria, demographics, comparator, site availability, external validity. |
| Endpoint mismatch | Surrogate or short-term endpoints may not equal durable patient benefit. | Pre-specified endpoint, clinical importance, independent replication, postapproval evidence. |
| Access and treatment burden | Complex procedures may be unavailable or unaffordable. | Eligibility, geography, cost, donor/cell supply, patient-reported burden. |

## Research priorities for Sprint 01

1. Reconcile each active registry with its protocol and linked results/publications.
2. Compare replacement strategies by insulin independence, C-peptide, severe hypoglycemia, glycemic outcomes, immunosuppression, adverse events, and follow-up.
3. Separate immune-evasive allogeneic donor cells from stem-cell-derived products; do not infer between them.
4. Build an explicit failure/negative-results ledger for encapsulation and immune modulation.
5. Define a minimum “functional cure” outcome set and avoid using the term for studies that do not meet it.
